Identity is the foundation of trust in our digital world. It’s how banks, governments, and website owners prove who you say you are. As online laws tighten around the globe and fraud worsens, there’s an ever-greater chance you’ll be asked to provide important identification documents like passports as proof of ID.

But how do you know that information will be properly protected? And what are the risks if something goes wrong? This isn’t theoretical. In May 2026, third-party site the [UK Visa Portal exposed](https://www.euroshub.com/blogs/uk-visa-portal-data-leak-engineering-lessons) at least 100,000 passports and selfies belonging to users.

## What’s the risk of uploading my ID?

Once you hand over your details to a third party, they could theoretically be breached by a threat actor, or accidentally exposed to the public through negligence. That’s what happened in the case of the UK Visa Portal, and a separate leak of personal data belonging to nearly [one million cannabis users](https://hightimes.com/news/cannaleaks-nearly-one-million-cannabis-club-users-data-was-exposed/) earlier this year.

In the UK Visa Portal incident, passport numbers, full names, dates of birth, contact info, passport scans and selfies were exposed. That could provide a fraudster with a wealth of personally identifiable information (PII) with which to commit identity fraud, unlock your financial accounts, or craft convincing phishing attacks to elicit more sensitive info.

## How do I minimize these risks?

There’s no way you can prevent an organization succumbing to a data breach. But there are steps you can take to minimize risk.

· First, double check that the document in question is definitely required. There may be other ways to authenticate, and there may be a way to share some but not all of the details on the document.

· You should also check that the site or portal you’re uploading to is secure and that the domain is correct, in case you have been redirected to a copycat phishing site. Try to use the official site rather than an intermediary.

· Never send sensitive information via email or messaging apps. For peace of mind, consider reading the small print of the privacy notice to check how long your PII will be stored for and what the mechanism is to have it deleted/removed.

### Are age verification sites also risky?

As regulations tighten and new rules emerge, [age verification](https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/social-media/time-internet-services-adopt-identity-verification/) for adult content, social media and other websites is becoming more widespread. But many users are understandably concerned about handing their PII over to a third-party verification provider. After [new rules came into force](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/age-checks-for-online-safety--what-you-need-to-know-as-a-user) in the UK, a surge in searches for VPNs was driven in part by privacy concerns over sharing sensitive information.

The identification documents and selfies you are forced to share with these providers make them a popular target for hackers. Biometrics like face scans are particularly risky as, unlike a passport number, this isn’t data that you can simply replace.

For the same reason, you may be nervous about using services like Tinder Face Check. Although the company says it deletes video selfies “promptly” after assessment, it keeps a “facial map and numerical face template” for the duration of your account. And it retains two “audit images” for 90 days after account closure. It also shares these with third parties.

If you’re unsure about sharing your PII with an age-verification provider, check the reputation of the company first, understand what data it collects and how much it retains, and find out whether it shares that information with any third party. It’s also worth knowing whether you can prove your age without needing to share PII.

## Beware of fake visa and travel permit sites

Not all websites requesting your PII have your best interests at heart. Some are deliberately designed to mimic legitimate sites and harvest your personal and financial details for use in follow-on fraud. You might reach them by clicking on a phishing link or a malicious link in the search rankings.

Always verify the domain you’re visiting before sharing any info, and use web security from a trusted provider to block visits to suspicious sites.

## What should I do if my ID was in a breach?

Don’t panic if you get a breach notification message. Confirm what was taken, change any passwords you may have shared and switch on two-factor authentication. If financial details were taken, contact your bank or card provider to freeze your account and replace any compromised cards, and monitor for suspicious activity. Have the document in question updated/replaced, and be on the lookout for suspicious communications.

Consider reporting the incident to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) and/or the ICO (UK), the [FTC](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) (US), or the [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/about-edpb/our-members_en), which can advise you of the relevant European regulator to contact.
