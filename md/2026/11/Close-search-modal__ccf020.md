## Executive Summary

Unit 42 has uncovered a multi-stage cyberespionage campaign that has been targeting Southeast Asian government entities since at least July 2025. We track this activity as [TGR-STA-0049](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/advanced-backdoor-squidoor/) (also tracked as REF7707, Earth Alux, Ink Dragon). The defining signature of the threat actor behind the activity is the targeting of government entities, combined with the use of advanced custom payloads that are designed for high-stealth intelligence collection operations, with an emphasis on evading security detection tools.

The campaign we investigated demonstrates high operational stealth and novel tool sets.

Activity associated with this group previously included deploying Squidoor (AKA [FINALDRAFT](https://malpedia.caad.fkie.fraunhofer.de/details/win.finaldraft)) – an advanced backdoor used against high-value global targets since 2023.

Recently, activity associated with this group has included expanded tradecraft. Associated activity that we observed included deploying two previously undocumented custom remote access Trojans (RATs): **PerfectPioneer RAT** (spotted in July 2025) and its modular successor, **KUKURAT** (deployed in March 2026).

Over a nine-month period, we observed the attackers behind the activity consistently refining their capabilities to meet evolving operational needs. This campaign’s tradecraft features three particularly advanced capabilities:

-   **Covert HTTP/3 (QUIC) C2 communications:** An early adoption of HTTP/3 over UDP, providing a highly efficient command and control channel that may bypass traditional TCP-optimized deep packet inspection.
-   **An operational safety switch:** PerfectPioneer RAT features a built-in validation engine that actively blocks remote operators from executing noisy or destructive commands that could trigger environment security alerts.
-   **Multi-vector heuristic camouflage:** KUKURAT employs a custom spoofing engine that generates synthetic, benign-looking network, registry and file activity. By manipulating the behavioral baseline of the endpoint, it can successfully obscure indicators of compromise within a vast volume of artificial noise.

## Background on TGR-STA-0049

TGR-STA-0049 has been active since at least March 2023. It primarily targets high-value sectors, including government organizations across Southeast Asia, South America and Europe. The targeting of strategic entities for data collection and exfiltration, combined with advanced stealth practices and long-term persistence, are characteristic of cyberespionage operations.

In previous campaigns, this group deployed [Squidoor](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/advanced-backdoor-squidoor/), a backdoor targeting Windows and Linux systems. As the group expanded its operations, it continued to [integrate new tools and malware variants](https://research.checkpoint.com/2025/ink-dragons-relay-network-and-offensive-operation/) into its tool set. In their most recent campaign, dating back to mid-2025, the attackers introduced two previously undocumented, advanced malware families designed for stealth, data collection and exfiltration: PerfectPioneer RAT and KUKURAT. As this campaign progressed into early 2026, the threat actor began transitioning their infrastructure from PerfectPioneer to the new KUKURAT, which has been observed in the wild since March 2026.

We uncovered six distinct variants of PerfectPioneer RAT that the group tracks as:

-   newhttp3
-   PerfectRat
-   Pioneer
-   RatAAkk-tt
-   rat_http3_ls
-   rat_http3_cc

All six variants have evolved iteratively since at least July 2025, exhibiting rapid, adaptive development cycles. Our analysis focuses on newhttp3 as a representative example.

### Infection Vectors

The attackers behind this activity leverage a dual-track infection strategy to establish and maintain a foothold inside targeted organizations through a combination of server exploitation and phishing.

Server exploitation is the primary infection vector observed in this campaign — such as the exploitation of vulnerable, unpatched Microsoft Exchange servers and web servers to deploy web shells. We observed a mix of custom and publicly available web shells, including variations of ASPXSpy, Godzilla, Bysunue.aspx and Suo5. This tooling is consistent with the actor's [past campaigns](https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/advanced-backdoor-squidoor/).

### HTTP/3 Adoption

Both PerfectPioneer RAT and KUKURAT use HTTP/3 as the primary method for communicating with C2 infrastructure. While the adoption of HTTP/3 is rare across the broader malware landscape, its deployment in this campaign reflects an [emerging trend](https://socprime.com/active-threats/salat-stealer-analysis/) among advanced threat actors. The deployment of HTTP/3 provides:

-   **A secure and fast communication channel:** HTTP/3 uses the [QUIC](https://www.checkpoint.com/cyber-hub/network-security/what-is-quic/) transport protocol running over UDP.
-   **An effective defense evasion mechanism:** Because HTTP/3 uses UDP, enterprise network defenses cannot perform passive TLS interception without breaking the underlying connection. Some corporate firewalls and traditional IDS/IPS engines are not updated to inspect UDP traffic with the same level of scrutiny as standard TCP traffic. When legacy security policies are not [optimized](https://sanjeev41924.medium.com/http-3-challenges-to-security-and-possible-response-e81f429506e0) exclusively for TCP-based web channels, outbound UDP port 443 communications frequently bypass deep packet inspection entirely – potentially allowing malicious QUIC traffic to flow unhindered.

## PerfectPioneer RAT: Deep Dive

Since at least July 2025, the group associated with this activity has deployed PerfectPioneer RAT, a custom [Go-based](https://go.dev/#) malware family. We examine the RAT's execution methods on infected hosts and explore the architecture and behavior of a representative variant. Our structural comparison of six distinct iterations illustrates the adversary's active development pipeline.

### Loaders and Execution

In addition to being dropped to disk as an executable, we observed the execution of PerfectPioneer RAT via three methods across compromised networks: DLL side-loading, decoy installers and phishing. This tactical versatility underscores the threat actor’s operational agility, optimizing target deception and defense evasion across varied environments.

-   **DLL side-loading**: The attackers dropped a legitimate, benign executable that they had structurally modified to contain XOR-encrypted shellcode embedded with the PerfectPioneer RAT payload. A malicious loader DLL accompanies this altered binary. Upon execution, the modified legitimate application loads the side-loaded DLL, which reads, decrypts and executes the shellcode from the tampered host executable. The shellcode then deploys the payload in memory.
-   **Decoy installers**: The actor used decoy software installers to drop to disk and execute the PerfectPioneer RAT background agent, while running a seemingly normal application setup wizard. In one case, we found that a decoy software installer wrote to disk and executed a [GlobalProtect](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/sase/globalprotect) installer. This legitimate Palo Alto Networks secure remote access product was used as a cover, while an executable of PerfectPioneer RAT was written to disk and executed in the background.
-   **Phishing**: Unsuspecting targets downloaded several malware samples from domains masquerading as official government websites. To evade detection and deceive victims, these executable files are disguised as PDF files, displaying a PDF icon. When the victim attempts to open the PDF file, they actually click on the malware which launches a decoy PDF document to maintain the illusion of legitimacy while the malware runs in the background.

### The newhttp3 Variant

Our deep dive focuses primarily on a variant that the author labeled newhttp3, as revealed in one of the recovered project paths: D:/proj/http3/newhttp3/agent/. We selected this sample for deeper technical evaluation because it represents the most feature-complete variant.

### Commands and Capabilities

The newhttp3 variant contains an expansive array of commands that potentially grant the remote operator total control over the compromised endpoint. These capabilities are natively compiled into the Go binary, unlike the dynamic modules architecture later introduced in KUKURAT.

newhttp3 includes a diverse suite of capabilities for comprehensive data harvesting, host control and resource management, which can be structurally grouped into the following functional categories:

-   File operations
    -   List, upload, download, delete: Full file system access
    -   Chunked transfers: Large files are transferred in chunks with progress tracking
    -   Temporary files management: Open file handles are tracked and periodically cleaned up
-   Shell execution
    -   Command execution capabilities
-   Process management
    -   List/kill/start processes
    -   COM-based process creation: Alternative execution path via [COM objects](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/com/component-object-model--com--portal)
    -   Code injection using the classic [VirtualAllocEx](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/memoryapi/nf-memoryapi-virtualallocex), [WriteProcessMemory](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/memoryapi/nf-memoryapi-writeprocessmemory) and [CreateRemoteThread](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/processthreadsapi/nf-processthreadsapi-createremotethread) techniques
-   Keylogging
-   SOCKS5 proxy
-   Screenshot capture
-   Window enumeration and manipulation
-   Resource monitoring

#### Architecture and Communication

The newhttp3 variant’s communication framework appears to be designed with a focus on both network evasion and operational stability. Rather than relying on a single communication channel, the malware blends stealthy network protocols with automated routines aimed at maintaining steady connectivity and managing host resource overhead.

PerfectPioneer RAT relies on HTTP/3 as its primary C2 communication mechanism, automatically falling back to HTTP/2 in the event of connection failure.

The agent interacts with the C2 infrastructure via four principal endpoints, as detailed in Table 1. The URL endpoints used by PerfectPioneer RAT overlap with those used later by KUKURAT, representing another key artifact connecting the two families to a common development pipeline.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| **URL Endpoint** | **Usage** |
| /agent/register | Register a new agent |
| /agent/heartbeat | Send heartbeat to C2 |
| /agent/tasks | Get tasks from C2 |
| /agent/result | Send task execution result |

Table 1. URL endpoints used by PerfectPioneer RAT newhttp3 variant.

#### Self-Healing Connection Architecture

PerfectPioneer RAT implements three highly specific automated routines. These are designed to maintain stable connections and manage system overhead, to minimize its forensic footprint and maintain a low profile.

-   **Reconnection routine**: This routine ensures the connection to the C2 remains active by tracking an internal variable named connected. If the connected variable is toggled to false by either the heartbeat or command polling routines due to a network disruption, the reconnection routine immediately steps in. It cancels the active heartbeat and command polling loops, then forces a clean re-registration workflow with the C2 server in an attempt to forcefully reset the connection state.
-   **Cleanup routine**: Designed to limit the malware's forensic footprint on disk and prevent excessive consumption of host memory, this routine achieves its goals via two automated methods:
    -   **Cleanup temporary files**: Continuously tracks and deletes all temporary files created during operation. This minimizes the creation of persistent artifacts on disk and shortens the window of opportunity for endpoint detection tools or forensic analysts to recover cached operational data from the file system.
    -   **Cleanup memory usage**: Monitors the active memory footprint of the RAT. If memory usage exceeds 100 MB, it explicitly forces Go’s runtime [garbage collection](https://go.dev/doc/gc-guide) cycle to aggressively purge unallocated memory. Keeping the process memory footprint low reduces the likelihood of triggering suspicion due to high CPU usage.
-   **Resource monitoring routine**: This routine tracks client-side CPU and memory utilization on behalf of the operator. It captures a performance snapshot every 30 seconds, which the remote operator can later retrieve via a specific command to monitor host machine overhead.

**The isDangerousCommand Foolproof Mechanism**

isDangerousCommand is one of the most notable and unprecedented features uncovered during our analysis. It is implemented in two specific variants that the author named Pioneer and rat_http3_cc.

Before executing any incoming command from the C2, the isDangerousCommand routine cross-references the instruction against a list of hard-coded commands. If it finds a match, the command is blocked entirely. The blocklist includes administrative commands that would be highly destructive or forensics-heavy (noisy) if executed and which security tools could potentially monitor. The inclusion of this operational “safety switch” prevents the operators from making mistakes that could result in the activity being detected.

Our analysis revealed that the hard-coded blocklist includes a mix of both Windows and Linux environment syntax, as Figure 3 shows.

## KUKURAT: A Deep Dive into the Latest Modular Backdoor

In March 2026, the threat actor introduced a new custom payload explicitly named KUKURAT into multiple networks that had already been compromised by PerfectPioneer RAT.

Written in C++, we identified two variants of KUKURAT in the wild. While their core functionality is similar, the variants use different underlying network protocols to communicate with the C2: one variant leverages HTTP/2, the other uses HTTP/3.

Unlike PerfectPioneer RAT, KUKURAT is a completely modular malware framework. Almost every post-exploitation capability is implemented as a separate DLL module loaded dynamically at runtime directly from the C2 server. KUKURAT’s code supports at least 12 distinct named modules.

By pulling capabilities directly into memory only when needed, KUKURAT achieves a high level of evasiveness and adaptability. Because the static core binary contains no advanced feature code, it successfully evades standard binary scans, simplifies feature updates and prevents forensic analysts from recovering the underlying payloads.

### Registration and Heartbeat

On startup, KUKURAT registers with its C2 infrastructure by connecting to a /register node and posting the following system information from the infected endpoint:

-   Hostname
-   Username and integrity level
-   OS type and architecture
-   Host IP address
-   Agent instance ID (randomly generated at runtime)

The C2 responds with initial runtime configuration for the agent.

Due to its highly modular architecture, KUKURAT’s main binary lacks the embedded code required to perform post-exploitation activities. ### Deployment of Separate KUKURAT Modules

During our investigation, we observed the attackers using KUKURAT to deploy two additional executables named PsExecService.exe and SecurityGuard.exe. While we could not retrieve SecurityGuard.exe, we successfully recovered PsExecService.exe for analysis.

According to the file's compiled program database (PDB) string, the attackers explicitly classify this binary as an external module of KUKURAT, despite it being compiled as a standalone executable rather than a DLL:

d:\\proj\\ **KUKURAT** \\ **modules** \\MyPsExec-master\\PsExecService\\

This tool is a custom remote execution service that appears may be partially based on the open-source [MyPsExec](https://github.com/zesiar0/MyPsExec/tree/master) GitHub project. Upon execution, PsExecService.exe registers itself as a local Windows service named PsExec and creates a named pipe called \\\\.\\pipe\\PSEXEC. The service natively supports four commands:

-   Shell execution
-   File uploading
-   File downloading
-   Directory listing

To limit its forensic footprint, the service employs an internal self-destruct timer that automatically uninstalls the service and cleans up its artifacts after five minutes of inactivity.

### Spoofing Endpoint Activity: Heuristic Camouflage Engine

KUKURAT introduces a unique heuristic camouflage engine capable of operating across three system axes: network, registry and files. The purpose of this engine is to generate dummy, legitimate-looking activity on the endpoint to camouflage the malware's actual operations. By mimicking normal operational behavior, the threat actor establishes a false baseline, which may significantly affect automated detection and other forensic efforts. The threat actor can toggle each method on or off via the C2 command set_obfuscation:

-   **Network noise generation**: KUKURAT generates 10 decoy HTTP requests per operational cycle to randomly selected domains, pulled from a hard-coded list of 15 popular websites using randomly generated URL paths. This camouflages traffic, blending C2 communications into normal web noise.
-   **Registry manipulation mimicking browser activity**: KUKURAT mimics legitimate Google Chrome browser behavior by writing random data to the registry path HKCU\\Software\\Google\\Chrome\\BrowserCrashRecovery, reading the data back, and then deleting the key. It then opens and enumerates the HKCU\\Software\\Google\\Update\\ClientState registry key. The purpose of this sequence is to make the agent's registry operation appear like Chrome crash recovery.
-   **File noise generation**: The malware continuously creates temporary files with random names and randomized content inside the system %temp% directory, only to immediately delete them. This technique generates file system noise, masking the agent's file modifications.

While certain elements of this evasion technique have been previously documented across various malware families, KUKURAT distinguishes itself through a highly unique design choice. It can coordinate heuristic camouflage across three separate vectors, while dynamically enabling or disabling each feature at runtime.

### The Watchdog Component

In multiple incidents, attackers deployed KUKURAT alongside a separate watchdog executable that ensures the malware remains active.

When KUKURAT’s main binary executes, it creates a dedicated thread to communicate with the companion watchdog component. KUKURAT writes its own active file path to a local named pipe called WINDOW_SEC_WATCHDOG, every three seconds. The companion watchdog process reads this path and pulls a heartbeat message every second. If 15 seconds pass without receiving a heartbeat, the watchdog assumes that KUKURAT has been terminated and automatically relaunches it using the cached file path.

On one occasion, we observed the threat actor deploying this watchdog component into the C:\\ProgramData\\Cyvera\\Ransomware\\ directory path. This folder structure was historically associated with [Cortex XDR](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/cortex/cortex-xdr), demonstrating the threat actor’s attempt to lower suspicion by hiding within security-product directory structures.

### Comparison of Variants

The investigation identified two distinct variants of KUKURAT, leveraging HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 respectively for C2 communications. Aside from the difference in network transport protocols, the two variants are structurally almost identical except for a few noteworthy code developments that affect the malware's development adaptability and operational survivability:

-   **Command-handling architecture:** In the HTTP/2 variant, the threat actor implemented a large, nested If-Else conditional chain inside a single command-handling function. All execution and module-loading logic is embedded directly within those conditions. The HTTP/3 variant demonstrates the transition to an array of function pointers that map to individual commands, creating a standalone function for each execution routine. This design shift enables the malware developers to add or remove capabilities on the fly more efficiently.
-   **Built-in exec bypass command**: While KUKURAT is engineered to function as a completely modular malware framework that loads code dynamically, the HTTP/3 variant introduces an explicit, natively coded command called exec. This command enables the attacker to spawn a subprocess directly on the host endpoint via [CreateProcessA](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/processthreadsapi/nf-processthreadsapi-createprocessa) and pipe the resulting terminal output back to the C2 backend. Because this command does not exist in the HTTP/2 variant, its inclusion suggests that the author sought to provide a built-in survival option: allowing operators to run system commands natively without needing to fetch an external module DLL from the staging server.

### Credential Stealing Techniques

Upon gaining initial access, the threat actor employs multiple techniques to harvest credentials including Local Security Authority Subsystem Service (LSASS) memory dumping and Directory Service Replication (DCSync).

The threat actor also actively used a robust suite of credential-dumping tools, dual-use administrative tools and registry manipulation techniques, including:

-   [Mimikatz](https://attack.mitre.org/software/S0002/) and [ProcDump](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sysinternals/downloads/procdump)
-   [SharpDPAPI](https://github.com/GhostPack/SharpDPAPI), to decrypt DPAPI credentials and secrets
-   Manually dumping the SAM and SYSTEM registry hives to extract local account hashes

#### LSASS Memory Dumping

The attackers leveraged a living off the land [technique](https://lolbas-project.github.io/lolbas/Libraries/comsvcs/) to dump the memory of the lsass.exe process. They misused the native comsvcs.dll Windows utility using the following command.

|     |     |
| --- | --- |
| 1   | CmD.exe /Q /c for /f "tokens=1,2 delims= " %A in ('"tasklist /fi "Imagename eq lsass.exe" \| find "lsass""') do rundll32.exe C:\\windows\\System32\\comsvcs.dll, #+000024 %B \\Windows\\Temp\\MxA.tar full |

Instead of calling the MiniDump function by name, the attackers invoked it via its ordinal hidden index number, #+000024. This evasion tactic is designed to bypass security detection mechanisms that look for the explicit MiniDump string.

### Data Exfiltration

After systematically moving through the network and harvesting high-privilege credentials, the threat actor initiated their primary objective: cyberespionage. Leveraging the PerfectPioneer RAT and KUKURAT malware families, the threat actor attempted to exfiltrate vast quantities of documents back to their C2 infrastructure. The targeted data predominantly consists of.pdf,.docx,.xlsx and.png files with filenames that suggest potential sensitive information in their content,with a pronounced focus on international relations and internal political affairs.

Beyond document exfiltration, the attackers also targeted email infrastructure. From their initial foothold on a compromised Exchange server, they executed various PowerShell commands to identify and misuse administrative privileges.

-   **Reconnaissance for impersonation rights:** The attackers first checked for accounts assigned the ApplicationImpersonation role. In Microsoft Exchange, this role allows a single account to impersonate any user (or a specific subset of users) in the organization. By leveraging this privilege, an account can access other mailboxes directly without needing to know or supply the explicit passwords of those individual users.

## Conclusion

The operations of TGR-STA-0049 reveal a highly adaptive threat actor targeting government infrastructure across Southeast Asia. Through the deployment of KUKURAT and the PerfectPioneer RAT, the attackers behind this activity are actively evolving to stay ahead of traditional network defenses by implementing protocol-level evasion via HTTP/3 and QUIC over UDP.

What makes this group particularly evasive is the extensive effort invested in maintaining operational security and remaining hidden on compromised endpoints. From the implementation of PerfectPioneer RAT’s automated self-healing connection architectures to KUKURAT's highly sophisticated heuristic camouflage engine, the group demonstrates a mature understanding of modern defensive ecosystems. They have even gone so far as to embed hard-coded administrative safety switches within their binaries to prevent their own operators from running loud or destructive commands that might trigger security alerts.

Ultimately, the group’s stealthy tradecraft serves a broader objective: ensuring the success of a highly targeted data exfiltration campaign. This was characterized by the actor’s widespread attempts to steal what appears to be sensitive intelligence, based on filenames, and to exploit mailbox impersonation rights across compromised Exchange environments.

To counter this advanced generation of espionage, defenders must look past legacy, TCP-centric visibility. Protecting high value networks requires a comprehensive shift toward capturing unauthorized UDP-based web traffic, strictly auditing Active Directory validation mechanisms and monitoring for host-level behavioral anomalies that these stealth focused campaigns leave behind.

### Palo Alto Networks Protection and Mitigation

To better protect against the TTPs used by TGR-STA-0049, organizations should consider implementing the following network-level and host-level behavioral controls:

-   **Monitor or restrict QUIC and force protocol fallback:** Network administrators should configure edge firewalls to either log and inspect QUIC traffic or block outbound UDP port 443 altogether. Blocking forces HTTP/3 and QUIC communications to gracefully fall back to TCP-based HTTP/2 or TLS channels, ensuring standard deep-packet inspection and decryption engines can monitor the traffic natively.
-   **Audit active directory log-on scripts:** Secure and continuously audit the sysvol share and Group Policy Objects for unauthorized modifications. Configure EDR platforms to alert on suspicious processes executing with domain user SIDs during user authentication.
-   **Monitor blind LSASS dumping methods:** Create behavioral detection rules tracking rundll32.exe interacting with comsvcs.dll to capture ordinal-based invocation methods (such as #+000024) that bypass standard string-based text filtering.
-   **Audit exchange impersonation and access permissions:** Regularly execute validation scripts to review accounts assigned the ApplicationImpersonation role. Monitor Exchange logs for the execution of Add-MailboxPermission commands, specifically flagging automated loops that grant an account FullAccess privileges across the organization.

**Project Path**: D:/proj/http3/Pioneer/

### Commands Blocked by isDangerousCommand

The isDangerousCommand foolproof mechanism uses a three-layered protection model to block categories and commands:

**Layer 1: Hard-Coded Keyword Filters**

This layer blocks a total of 41 explicit destructive, system modification and administrative commands attempted by the operator:

-   **Disk destruction:  
    **Blocks commands that format, wipe or overwrite raw storage.  
    Keywords: format, fdisk, mkfs, dd if=, diskpart, cipher /w:, sdelete, wipe, shred

-   **Recursive deletion:  
    **Prevents aggressive, unconstrained file deletion commands.  
    Keywords: rm -rf /, del /f /s /q c:

-   **System power actions:**  
    Intercepts commands that would shut down or restart the host, killing the agent's active session.  
    Keywords: shutdown, reboot, halt, poweroff, init 0, init 6

-   **Denial of service (fork bomb):**  
    Blocks the classic Linux shell resource exhaustion string.  
    Keywords::(){:|:& };:

-   **Permissions and ownership tampering:**  
    Blocks broad access modifications targeting root structures.  
    Keywords: chmod 777 /, chown root /

-   **User and group management:**  
    Prevents the rogue manipulation, addition, or removal of system accounts.  
    Keywords: passwd, su -, sudo su, userdel, useradd, usermod, groupdel, groupadd

-   **Service disruption and process killing:**  
    Filters commands designed to terminate background tasks or wipe automated execution schedules.  
    Keywords: crontab -r, systemctl stop, systemctl disable, service stop, killall, pkill -9, kill -9 1

-   **Firewall disruption:**  
    Blocks commands that attempt to flush or reset host-based firewall configurations.  
    Keywords: iptables -F, ufw --force reset, netsh firewall

-   **Registry and boot configuration manipulation:**  
    Intercepts Windows-specific administrative tweaks that alter system states or delete sensitive hives.  
    Keywords: reg delete, regedit, bcdedit

**Layer 2: Malicious Execution Pattern Filters**

This layer acts as a heuristic filter, blocking 16 common payload delivery shortcuts, obfuscation attempts and command chaining:

-   **Chained deletions:**  
    Detects attempts to append file removal commands onto logical execution operators.  
    Patterns: && rm, | rm,; rm, && del, | del,; del
-   **Direct device writes:**  
    Blocks direct outputs to raw Linux terminal paths or data redirection loops designed to suppress error messages.  
    Patterns: > /dev/, 2>/dev/null
-   **Download-and-execute pipelines:**  
    Intercepts classic script-staging methods that download and immediately pipe online payloads into a local shell.  
    Patterns: curl | sh, wget | sh, curl | bash, wget | bash
-   **Encoded PowerShell execution:**  
    Blocks attempts to run heavily obfuscated, Base64-encoded PowerShell payloads natively.  
    Patterns: powershell -enc, powershell -e
-   **Silent string echoing:**  
    Prevents script manipulation shortcuts or automated interactive prompting bypasses.  
    Patterns: cmd /c echo, echo y |

**Layer 3: Core Directory Staging Protections**

The final layer serves as a localized guardrail. It explicitly drops any file removal commands (rm, del, rmdir, rd) if they are directed at one of 12 critical operating system paths required to keep the endpoint stable and online:

-   **Linux protected directories**:  
    /etc/, /bin/, /sbin/, /usr/bin/, /usr/sbin/, /boot/, /sys/, /proc/
-   **Windows protected directories**:  
    c:\\windows, c:\\program files, c:\\program files (x86), c:\\system volume information

### PerfectPioneer RAT Persistence

PerfectPioneer RAT establishes long-term persistence on infected hosts via two concurrent methods:

1.  **Registry Run key**: Directly writes the executable's absolute path to HKCU\\Software\\Microsoft\\Windows\\CurrentVersion\\Run
2.  **Startup folder shortcut**: Creates a.lnk shortcut file inside the logged-in user's Startup folder.
