Scammers are past masters at [using our humanity](https://www.welivesecurity.com/2022/05/12/10-reasons-why-we-fall-scams/) against us. That’s why they deploy social engineering tactics like urgency or FOMO to rush us into poor decision making. And it’s why they weaponize our innate curiosity to make their one-ring (“wangiri”) scams work so well.

Wangiri fraud is happening increasingly frequently. Nearly half (45%) of global carriers polled by the [Global Leaders' Forum](https://contents.comms.delinian.com/rs/462-OQR-635/images/GLF%20Fraud%20Report%202025.pdf?version=0) (GLF) last year said they’d experienced a high volume of this type of scam, up from 38% in 2021. The percentage reporting a high financial impact increased from 21% to 32% over the same period.

If the phone rang once and abruptly stopped, would you be tempted to call it back? There are a growing number of reasons to stop and think before you do so.

## How do one-ring scams work?

Wangiri is a Japanese term that’s a combination of the words “one ring” and “cut.” The scams combine number spoofing, spamming and international revenue share fraud (IRSF) to part you with your money. The scam itself is disarmingly simple:

1.  You receive a phone call from an unfamiliar number. It may feature an international dialling code or be spoofed to appear as a local number. It rings once before the caller hangs up abruptly, leaving no message.
2.  Perhaps you were waiting for an important call for work, or are simply curious to know who it was. Either way, your curiosity is piqued so you call back.
3.  The call connects to a premium-rate international number which immediately starts costing your money. You might be kept on the line with an automated message, potentially explaining how you could win a prize or receive an important delivery.
4.  The scammers receive a share of the profits generated by the premium number.

The extra money you end up paying, some of which will find its way into the pocket of the fraudster, comes from the premium-rate number and the fact it is likely to be an international call. For the scammers, automated software makes it easy to make these spam calls in large volumes at a relatively low cost. That means they only need a small percentage of recipients to fall for the scam to make it worth their while.

According to the GLF, the top two most targeted countries globally are the US and UK.

### Are international numbers always suspicious?

Not all calls from abroad should be treated with suspicion. You might receive legitimate incoming calls and messages from hotels and other businesses based overseas, or even local companies that use call centers abroad. The key is to spot the combination of warning signs: an unfamiliar number, an abrupt end to the connection, and potentially a string of similar calls coming in from different numbers.

Bear in mind too that Calling Line Identification (CLI) or Caller ID spoofing is relatively easier for fraudsters to achieve today. This will enable them to show their international call as coming from a local number, which could persuade you to call back.

### Are wangiri calls getting more popular?

One-call scams have been around for years. The FCC [issued a warning](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-alerts-consumers-one-ring-call-back-scam) about them as far back as 2019. But they appear to be growing in popularity. That’s because campaigns are relatively cheap and easy to operate, generating a decent RoI even if only a small number of victims call back. They can also be difficult for carriers to trace and block, given that international calls are often routed via various third parties. And if one number does get blocked, it’s simple for the fraudster to move onto another.

To make their scam more convincing, fraudsters could follow up with a text message, using that old social engineering tactic of creating urgency to persuade you to call back. It could be something good (a time-limited offer or prize) or something bad (a fine or threat if you don’t call back). There are [also reports](https://globaltelcoconsult.com/introduction-to-wangiri) that scammers are targeting businesses to generate automated callbacks, making them even more money before a human notices what’s happening.

## How can I stay safe from missed call scams?

Operators are doing their best to improve validation of traffic and spot unusual call patterns, using AI-powered analytics. But sufficient international collaboration is still often lacking at a scale that could make a real impact on spam call volumes. That puts the onus on you to protect yourself from one-ring phone scams.

Consider the following:

-   If an unknown number calls and then stops after one or two rings, don’t call it back
-   Check the number with a Google search to see if it is legitimate or associated with scams. However, be aware that scam numbers are constantly changing, so it may not have garnered any bad reviews at the time you get called
-   Block it on your phone. Some manufacturers like Apple now flag possible spam calls automatically
-   Check your phone bill to make sure there are no unexpected international or premium-rate call charges on there
-   File a [complaint with the FCC](http://www.fcc.gov/complaints) if you’ve started receiving a high volume of these calls

### I’ve already call back: what now?

Don’t panic. Hang up the call immediately. Contact your mobile provider to look for suspicious premium-rate/international call charges and dispute them as fraud. If you handed over any sensitive personal or financial information, contact your bank and tell them what happened. They will be able to advise you how to secure your accounts. If you gave away a password, change it immediately on any site/app you use it.

Phone-based scams are the price we pay for ubiquitous smartphone connectivity. If in doubt, ignore a caller you don’t recognize and check back once they’ve rung off.

## FAQ:

**What is a wangiri or one-ring phone scam?**

When scammers call you and hang up after a ring or two. The aim is usually for them to trick you into calling back and connecting to premium-rate numbers, which generates revenue for them.

**Should I call back a missed call from an unknown number?**

Never. It may be a scam. If the call is urgent the caller should leave you a voicemail. Always search for the number to see if it is legitimate first.

**Can a missed call cost me money?**

No. Only if you pick up the phone. It’s always worth looking at your bill though to double check.

**Why do I get calls that ring once? Should I call back an international number?**

Never call back an international number that you don’t recognize and which has rung only a couple of times before hanging up. It’s likely to be a scam.

**What if I called back a one-ring number?**

You’ll be connected to a premium-rate (and usually international) number which could cost you a great deal of money.

**How do I know if a missed call is a scam?**

There are a few red flags. It is an unfamiliar, often international number. It will have rung only once or twice before hanging up. And it may be one of several similar calls coming from different numbers.

**Can I be charged just for receiving a one-ring scam call?**

Not if you haven’t picked up. However, it’s always worth double-checking with your mobile provider.

**Are international missed calls always scams?**

No, it could be a legitimate business trying to get in touch with you, like a hotel, or a domestic company with a call center abroad. Still, it’s always best not to answer if you’re unsure and do an online search for the number.
